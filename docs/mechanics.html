ZooMounter — Mechanics Reference

What the tool calculates, what it assumes, and where those assumptions break. Written as the questions came up while building against a real X/Y gantry, so it records mistakes as well as conclusions.

Working document. Last updated 2026-07-28.

1. Coordinate system and terminology

The mount is a thin square slab. Two dimensions are the face you bolt the motor to; the third is how thick that slab is — and the third one is what ZooMounter is solving for.

FieldAxisNEMA 23Role in the calculation
plate_width_mmX56.4 mmthe beam width w
plate_height_mmY56.4 mmnone — volume estimate only
thicknessZsolvedthe beam depth t

Naming trap. plate_height_mm is the in-plane Y dimension — not vertical height, and not thickness. It collides with beam theory, where a section's "height" is the dimension it bends through, which here is the thickness.

open On a square plate this is invisible. On a rectangular mount it matters which face dimension becomes w, and the code currently always picks plate_width_mm without checking which way the load acts.

2. The two load cases

Same 120 N force produces a 5× difference in required thickness. The difference is not the force — it is whether the force has a lever arm.

Radial — the plate bends

A belt or gear pulls sideways on the motor shaft. The load acts some distance from the plate, so it applies a bending moment. The plate is treated as a cantilever beam.

M = F × L
σ = 6M / (w·t²) → t = √(6M / w·σ)
δ = F·L³ / (3·E·I), I = w·t³/12, δ ≤ L/300
CheckResult
Stress (yield ÷ SF = 120 MPa)2.51 mm
Deflection (limit L/300 = 0.19 mm)4.97 mmgoverns

Stiffness governs, not strength. At 4.97 mm the plate is roughly twice as strong as it needs to be — the extra material is there to stop it flexing. If your application tolerates more deflection, this number drops quickly.

Axial — the plate barely cares

A leadscrew pushes along the shaft axis, straight into the plate face. No lever arm, no moment. The only plate-side failure mode is the screw head punching through, which needs 0.014 mm — physically meaningless, so the machining floor takes over at 1.00 mm.

A thin axial result is a warning, not a green light. It means the plate is not the weak link. See §4 — for real thrust loads the motor's own bearings almost always fail first, and ZooMounter does not check them.

3. Where the motor actually mounts bug

A NEMA motor bolts by its front faceplate — the end the shaft comes out of. The body hangs behind the plate; the pulley sits just in front of it. ZooMounter's default lever arm is the motor's body length, which measures backwards from the mount, in the wrong direction entirely.

Deflection-governed thickness scales as L2/3, so a realistic 15 mm arm gives 2.06 mm instead of 4.97 mm — the tool currently over-specifies by about 2.4×.

Self-weight is mis-placed too

The code adds the motor's weight to the belt force and applies both at the same arm. But the motor's centre of gravity is roughly half a body-length behind the plate, which is a different arm in a different direction.

ContributionForceArmMoment
Belt tension (shaft side)120 N15 mm1800 N·mm
Motor weight (body side)6.87 N28 mm192 N·mm
Correct total1992 N·mm
What the code computes126.87 N56 mm7105 N·mm — 3.6× high

Proposed fix. Split the single typical_body_length_mm into two independent distances, because they are genuinely two different things:

  • shaft_load_offset_mm — mount face to where the belt or gear load acts. Drives the radial bending moment.
  • body_cg_offset_mm — mount face to the motor's centre of gravity, behind the plate. Drives the self-weight moment.

Both are overridable, since the real number depends on the pulley you fit. --overhang-mm already overrides the first.

4. Axial loads: what actually carries thrust gap

ZooMounter answers "how thick should the plate be" and for axial thrust the honest answer is "thin — that isn't your problem." But it stops there, and the useful answer is what to do instead.

Stepper motors are not thrust bearings

A stepper's internal bearings are sized to locate the rotor, not to carry axial load. Manufacturers typically quote continuous axial ratings in the region of tens of newtons, and it is a number worth reading off the actual datasheet rather than assuming — it is easy to exceed with a leadscrew. Exceed it and the failure is progressive: bearing wear, then rotor rub, then lost steps.

The usual answers, roughly in order of cost

ApproachCarriesWhen it fits
Thrust washer / bronze facelight, intermittentlow speed, low duty, cheapest possible
Thrust ball bearingaxial onlypure thrust, no side load
Angular contact bearingaxial + radialleadscrews — the common right answer
Back-to-back angular contact pairthrust both directionsreversing loads, preloadable
Fixed/floating bearing pairthrust + thermal growthlong screws that expand in use

The important architectural point: with a proper thrust bearing, the load path no longer runs through the motor at all. The screw pushes the bearing, the bearing pushes its housing, the housing pushes the frame — and the motor only supplies torque through a coupling that deliberately tolerates float.

What ZooMounter could do about it. Not analysis — advice and geometry:

  • On any axial run, state the thrust load and prompt the user to check it against their motor's axial rating.
  • Add a bearing-housing mount type (the bearing_608 entry is the beginning of this, though a real one needs a shouldered pocket rather than a plain through-bore).
  • Suggest a bearing class from the load and whether it reverses.

None of this is FEA. It is the same domain-rules approach already used for bolt patterns, applied to a different decision.

5. Assumptions and limits

  • Hand-calc grade. Rectangular section, static load, no stress concentration at the holes. A sanity check, not FEA.
  • Fully fixed root. The plate is assumed rigidly built in at the frame. Real bolted joints have some compliance, so true deflection will be a little worse.
  • Deflection limit L/300 is a general bracket rule of thumb, currently hard-coded. For a belt drive the number that actually matters is how much misalignment throws the belt.
  • One load type per run. Radial and axial are not combined; run both and take the worse case.
  • Motor mass and dimensions are representative for a typical NEMA 17/23, not your specific motor.
  • No fatigue. Everything here is static. A gantry that reverses thousands of times a day is a fatigue problem, and this does not address it.
